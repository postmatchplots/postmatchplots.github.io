Guide  /  How the numbers work

What is xG?

Expected goals, or xG, is the chance that a shot goes in. A shot with 0.3 xG is the kind that gets scored about three times in ten. Add up every shot a team takes and you get how many goals their chances were worth, which tells you more about how they played than the score does.

A shot's worth, in one number

Every shot gets compared with hundreds of thousands of similar shots from the past. If shots from that spot, in that situation, went in 12% of the time, the shot is worth 0.12 xG. It's always somewhere between 0 (no chance) and 1 (can't miss).

The model behind the numbers on this site is Opta's. It looks at where the shot was taken from, the angle to goal, whether it was a header or a kick, what kind of pass set it up, and whether it came from open play, a set piece or a counter. Penalties are simple: they all get 0.79, because that's roughly how often they're scored.

Why it beats counting shots

A tap-in from two yards and a hopeful one from 35 yards both count as one shot. They are not the same thing. United have taken more shots than anyone this season, but nearly half came from outside the box, where each one is worth about a third of a normal chance. Their 203 shots add up to 18.2 xG; Man City's 165 add up to 21.9.

So xG is a better guide to goals than shots are. Put each team's xG next to the goals they've actually scored and most sit near the line where the two are equal.

What the gap between goals and xG means

Some teams finish above or below their xG. After 11 games that's mostly noise: shots go in or they don't, and a few lucky deflections can add up. Chelsea have scored 20 from chances worth 14.6; Forest 9 from 17.7. Neither is likely to keep that up for a whole season. Over a long stretch most teams drift back towards their xG, which is why it's handy for spotting who's been lucky and who's been hard done by.

A team or player who beats their xG season after season might genuinely be better at finishing. That's hard to prove from a few months of shots, so on this site a gap is treated as "probably luck" unless there's more evidence.

What xG doesn't tell you

It's about the chance, not the finish. xG is worked out from the moment the shot is taken. A shot that hits the top corner and one that's scuffed wide from the same spot get the same xG. (FotMob also shows "xGOT", xG on target, which does take account of where the shot went. This site doesn't use it.)

No shot, no xG. A cross that flashes across the six-yard box with nobody there to touch it creates nothing in xG, however close it came.

Small samples swing. One match's xG can be dominated by a single penalty. Over a few games it settles down; over a season it's much steadier.

It isn't the score. Losing 1-0 while "winning" 2.1 to 0.6 on xG means you played better, not that you deserved the points by right. Football is allowed to be unfair.

The family of x-stats

StatWhat it means
xGHow many goals a team's or player's shots were worth.
npxGNon-penalty xG: the same, with penalties taken out. Useful because who takes the penalties is a squad decision, not a sign of how well someone plays.
xGAxG against: how many goals the chances a team gave away were worth.
xAExpected assists: the xG of the shots a player sets up with his pass, whether or not they go in. Rewards the pass, not the finish.
Big chanceOpta's label for a chance a player would reasonably be expected to score, usually one-on-one or from very close in.
Expected pointsTreats every shot in a match as a roll of the dice to work out each side's chance of winning, then turns that into points. Worked through here.
